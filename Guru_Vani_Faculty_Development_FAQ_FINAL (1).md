# 1. About Guru Vani

## 1.1 What is Guru Vani?

Guru Vani is an online **Faculty Development Programme for higher-education faculty**. It focuses on practical areas of academic practice such as teaching, assessment, classroom engagement, professional judgement, and responding to real teaching situations.

The programme is designed to move beyond simply consuming content. Faculty members learn through coursework, live discussions, teaching cases, peer comparison, and a practical classroom project that can be taken back into their own teaching practice.

## 1.2 Who runs the programme?

Guru Vani is run by **VLED Lab, IIT Ropar**.

The programme is delivered online and is designed specifically around the professional context of higher-education faculty. The programme structure, participation requirements, and completion criteria are designed around the professional context of higher-education faculty.

## 1.3 How long is the programme?

Guru Vani runs for **8 weeks**.

It follows a **rolling-intake model**, which means your eight-week journey starts from your own start date rather than from a common academic calendar or a fixed cohort start. This makes the programme easier to join without requiring faculty members to wait for a later batch.

## 1.4 How much time should I plan for?

You should plan for approximately **4 hours per week** over the eight weeks.

Around **1.5 hours per week** are expected to be live with other faculty, while the remaining time is used for coursework, case activities, peer comparison, and project work. The programme represents approximately **33 notional learning hours** in total, so it is worth considering the time commitment before you begin.

## 1.5 Is Guru Vani a technical programme?

No. Guru Vani is designed as a **non-technical faculty development programme**.

The focus is on teaching practice, assessment, classroom situations, engagement with students and classroom learning, reflection, professional judgement, and practical improvement. You are not expected to come in as a programmer or technical specialist.

## 1.6 Why is Guru Vani designed around classroom application?

The programme is built around a simple question: **what will you actually use in your classroom after the programme?**

That is why the experience combines learning content with teaching cases, professional discussion, peer judgement, and a final classroom asset. The aim is not only to understand a teaching idea, but to think through how it applies to a real classroom and turn that learning into something usable.

## 1.7 Is Guru Vani meant to replace my normal teaching responsibilities?

No.

Participation is intended to happen **alongside your existing institutional responsibilities**. The programme is voluntary and in your own time, and it does not provide duty leave or relieve you from teaching, examination, or invigilation responsibilities.

This commitment is also reflected in the NOC that your institution signs, so it is important to make sure the programme fits realistically alongside your normal workload before you apply.

## 1.8 Why is the programme spread across eight weeks?

Guru Vani combines several different forms of learning rather than treating faculty development as a single workshop.

You may complete coursework asynchronously, attend live discussions, work through teaching cases, read and compare other faculty responses, revise your own thinking, and develop a classroom asset. Spreading these activities across eight weeks gives you time to apply ideas and engage with the process instead of trying to complete everything in one short session.

## 1.9 What is the relationship between the learning activities and the final classroom asset?

The final classroom asset is intended to be the **practical application of the programme**, not a separate academic assignment.

During the programme, you encounter ideas and classroom situations that can help you identify a genuine teaching problem. The final project gives you an opportunity to respond to that problem by creating something another teacher could understand and use in practice.

## 1.10 What does “professional judgement” mean in Guru Vani?

Professional judgement means being able to look at a teaching situation, consider different possible responses, and decide which response is stronger or more appropriate — with reasons.

Guru Vani develops this especially through the Activities component. You do not only write your own response; you also compare responses written by other faculty members and make a reasoned choice between them. The comparison is part of the learning, not just an evaluation step.

## 1.11 Is Guru Vani primarily a video course?

No.

The ViBe coursework is one part of Guru Vani, but it is **not the whole programme**. Completion also involves live standups, teaching-case activities, peer comparison, a practical project, and the required institutional NOC.

The programme is therefore better understood as a structured faculty-development journey with several connected components rather than a video library followed by a certificate.

## 1.12 Is the programme self-paced?

Partly.

The coursework and several activity components can be completed asynchronously within your eight-week journey, but Guru Vani also includes required live standups. Your deadlines and progress are calculated from your own start date, so you are not following someone else's fixed calendar week.

## 1.13 What makes Guru Vani different from a traditional FDP?

Guru Vani is designed around a more applied model of faculty development.

Instead of ending at “content completed,” the programme asks faculty to **participate, discuss, compare, revise, and create**. A successful experience should leave you with not only ideas about teaching, but also a clearer professional judgement about classroom situations and a practical asset that you can use.

## 1.14 Do I receive a certificate just for attending?

No.

The certificate is issued **on completion of the programme requirements, not simply on the basis of attendance**. You must complete the required coursework, standup participation, Activities, Project requirements, and have a verified NOC.

Once all required completion conditions are satisfied, the certificate is designed to be issued automatically.

# 2. Programme Structure

## 2.1 What are the four main parts of Guru Vani?

Guru Vani is organised into four programme verticals:

1. **Coursework** — complete the required Guru Vani course on ViBe.
2. **Standups** — participate in live faculty sessions, meet the attendance requirements, and take part in the live-session polls.
3. **Activities** — work through teaching cases, submit your own responses, and learn through peer comparison.
4. **Project** — create a practical classroom asset, have it peer-reviewed, and complete the required peer reviews.

In addition to these four verticals, **NOC verification is required for programme completion**.

## 2.2 What are the exact completion requirements?

To complete Guru Vani, all five requirements below must be met:

| Requirement | Completion condition |
|---|---|
| **Coursework** | ViBe course progress reaches **100%** |
| **Standups** | **720 minutes** of attendance across at least **12 sessions**, with active participation in the live-session polls |
| **Activities** | **12 cases** reach the **There yet** verdict |
| **Project** | Your artifact reaches **Ready**, and you complete **2 peer reviews** |
| **NOC** | Your NOC is **verified** |

These requirements are separate. Completing one component does not compensate for an incomplete component elsewhere.

## 2.3 Can I compensate for one incomplete vertical by doing extra work in another?

No. Each completion requirement must be met separately. For example, attending additional Standups cannot replace incomplete Coursework, and completing additional Activities cannot replace the Project requirement.

## 2.4 Is everything graded with marks?

No. Guru Vani does not use one overall numerical score to determine completion. Different parts of the programme use completion states, participation requirements, peer comparison, professional judgement, and peer-review outcomes.

## 2.5 Is a leaderboard or points-based ranking used for certification?

No. Certification is based on the published completion requirements, not on a leaderboard position, points total, or tier.

## 2.6 What other platform features support the programme?

The platform includes programme-support features such as:

- **Yaksha** for programme questions and the intake conversation
- **Discipline-specific forums** for faculty interaction
- **Announcements** and participant communications
- **Support and escalation routes**
- **Exit tickets** for feedback
- A participant **dashboard** showing progress across the programme

These features support the participant experience; they are not automatically separate certification requirements unless specifically listed in the completion rules.

## 2.7 Why are there separate verticals instead of one single completion score?

The four verticals represent different kinds of faculty development. Coursework supports structured learning; Standups provide sustained live participation; Activities build judgement through teaching cases and peer comparison; and the Project turns learning into a practical classroom asset.

This structure allows the programme to recognise more than one kind of participation instead of reducing the entire experience to a single test or attendance number.

## 2.8 What will I see on the dashboard when I start?

The dashboard is designed to show the full scope of the programme from the beginning. It should make your progress and remaining requirements visible across the four verticals, rather than revealing the programme requirements only at the end.

## 2.9 Can I complete the four verticals in any order?

You can work through the available components within your own eight-week timeline, but some activities naturally depend on earlier actions. For example, you cannot complete peer comparisons for a case before submitting your own response.

## 2.10 Are all four verticals required?

Yes. All four verticals are part of the programme completion requirements, along with verified NOC status.

## 2.11 What does “completion” mean in Guru Vani?

Completion means that **all five published requirements** have been met. It does not mean simply finishing the ViBe videos or attending enough live sessions.

## 2.12 What do 33 notional learning hours mean?

The 33 notional learning hours describe the overall learning volume of the programme. They are not a promise that a fixed number of hours will be spent on one specific activity each week; the time is distributed across Coursework, live participation, Activities, peer comparison, and the Project.

# 3. Who Can Join and How Admission Works

## 3.1 Who is Guru Vani intended for?

Guru Vani is designed specifically for **higher-education faculty** who want to strengthen their teaching practice, assessment approaches, classroom engagement, and professional judgement.

## 3.2 How do I apply?

You apply through the Guru Vani programme route on Samagama. The application is one continuous flow with three parts: **application form, Yaksha intake conversation, and required acknowledgements**. The application is complete only after all three are finished.

## 3.3 What information is collected in the application?

The application asks for your name, institution, department, designation, years of teaching experience, what you teach, and a short statement about why you want to participate.

## 3.4 Is the Yaksha conversation optional?

No. The intake conversation is a required part of the application and cannot simply be skipped and completed later.

## 3.5 Is Yaksha a test or interview?

No. The Yaksha intake conversation is **not a screening interview** and does not assign a merit score. Its purpose is to understand your teaching context and perspective.

## 3.6 How long does the Yaksha conversation take?

Plan for approximately **20–30 minutes**. It is an adaptive conversation rather than a fixed questionnaire.

## 3.7 Does Yaksha ask the same fixed questions to everyone?

No. Yaksha is designed to be adaptive. It follows up on what you say and concludes when it has enough understanding for the purpose of the conversation.

## 3.8 Can a weak answer cause me to be rejected?

No. Your answers are not scored for admission merit. A short answer may lead to a follow-up question, but the purpose is understanding rather than ranking.

## 3.9 Is there a human selection committee that approves every participant?

No. The normal admission flow is designed to be automatic once the required application conditions are satisfied.

## 3.10 Can I start before my NOC is verified?

Yes. After you have completed the application and Yaksha intake conversation, you can begin the programme while your NOC is being arranged.

## 3.11 What is my admission status while the NOC is pending?

You can participate in the programme while the NOC is pending. Once the NOC is verified, the NOC requirement is marked complete and your admission status can be updated accordingly.

## 3.12 Is Guru Vani a fixed cohort?

No. Guru Vani uses **rolling intake**. Your eight-week journey begins from your own start date rather than from a common cohort start date.

## 3.13 What happens after I submit the application form?

The application remains incomplete until the Yaksha conversation and required acknowledgements are completed. Once those steps are complete, you can proceed into the programme even if the NOC is still being arranged.

## 3.14 Why is the Yaksha conversation part of the application?

It gives the programme an early understanding of how faculty think about teaching, learning, and professional development. It is intended to build context for the programme rather than act as a selection interview.

## 3.15 What kind of topics can Yaksha ask me about?

The conversation can explore your teaching, learners, classroom context, research, professional-development expectations, and related areas. Because it is adaptive, the exact follow-up questions depend on what you say.

## 3.16 Can I prepare answers for the Yaksha conversation?

You do not need to prepare a “correct” answer. The programme is not looking for a fixed set of responses; it is trying to understand your context and perspective.

## 3.17 What if I give a short or incomplete answer?

Yaksha can ask a follow-up question. A short first response is not a reason for rejection; the purpose of the conversation is to build enough understanding for the programme.

## 3.18 Why does the conversation have a time cap?

The programme is designed for busy faculty members participating in their own time. The expected conversation is about 20–30 minutes, with a cap above that so it does not remain open-ended indefinitely.

## 3.19 What if Yaksha does not conclude the conversation correctly?

The programme design allows facilitator intervention in exceptional cases so that a technical or conversational problem does not prevent a faculty member from progressing.

## 3.20 What information does the programme actually use for admission?

Admission is based on required completion facts rather than the quality of your opinions. The intake conversation must be completed, and the NOC must eventually be verified for the participant to complete the admission requirements.

# 4. NOC — No Objection Certificate

## 4.1 Do I need an NOC?

Yes. A verified NOC is required for programme completion. The NOC confirms that your institution is aware of and supports your participation and that the time commitment is feasible alongside your existing responsibilities.

## 4.2 Who can sign the NOC?

The NOC may be signed by an appropriate institutional authority such as your **HOD, Dean, or Principal**, according to your institution's process.

## 4.3 What does the NOC confirm?

The NOC confirms, among other things, that your participation is acceptable to the institution and that the programme commitment is feasible alongside your normal duties. Your teaching, examination, and invigilation responsibilities continue as usual.

The signing authority is also asked to write a short paragraph explaining **why the faculty member is being supported and what they expect the faculty member to bring back to the department**.

## 4.4 Can I start the programme before my NOC is ready?

Yes. **You do not need to have the verified NOC before you begin the programme.** After completing the application and the Yaksha intake conversation, you can start your Guru Vani journey while the NOC is being arranged.

## 4.5 When should I submit the NOC?

You should try to arrange and submit the NOC **as early as possible**, preferably within the first **7 days** of your start. The seven-day period is a practical target for completing the institutional formalities, not a point after which your participation automatically stops.

## 4.6 What if my NOC is submitted after 7 days?

You can still submit it after the first seven days. A late NOC does not by itself cancel your participation or remove access to the programme. Please submit it as soon as possible and, if there is a delay at your institution, keep the programme team informed.

## 4.7 Does a late NOC affect my eight-week programme period?

No. Your eight-week journey continues from your original start date. Submitting the NOC late does not pause or extend the programme timeline.

## 4.8 What happens after my NOC is verified?

Your NOC status is updated to **verified**, and the NOC requirement is considered complete. There is no separate participant-side approval step that you need to request after submitting the document.

## 4.9 What if my NOC is returned for correction?

If the NOC is returned because a correction is needed, make the required correction and resubmit it. The important thing is to resolve the document as soon as possible so that your verified NOC is recorded during your programme.

## 4.10 What file format can I use for the NOC?

The programme supports a signed NOC uploaded as a **PDF or scan** through the NOC submission area.

## 4.11 Is there financial support, a stipend, or reimbursement?

No. The programme does not provide a stipend, honorarium, or reimbursement of programme-related costs.

## 4.12 Do I get duty leave or relief from teaching duties?

No. Participation is voluntary and takes place in your own time. The programme does not ask your institution to provide duty leave or relieve you from teaching, examination, or invigilation responsibilities.

## 4.13 Will my institution be kept informed?

Yes. Programme correspondence is copied to the certifying authority associated with the NOC, according to the programme communication policy. This can include reminders, status updates, and completion communication.

## 4.14 What happens if I withdraw or do not complete the programme?

Non-completion is recorded and may be taken into account when places are allocated in later cycles.

# 8. Vertical 2 — Standups

## 8.1 What are Standups?

Standups are **live online sessions for faculty members**. They are designed for discussion, participation, and sharing perspectives on teaching practice rather than being lecture-only sessions.

They give faculty an opportunity to learn from other faculty members, discuss classroom experiences, and engage with ideas introduced through the programme.

## 8.2 What is the attendance requirement?

To complete the Standup component, you need to:

1. Complete at least **720 minutes** of attendance across the programme.
2. Attend at least **12 Standup sessions**.
3. **Participate in the live-session polls** when they are presented during the sessions you attend.

The polls are used as an engagement mechanism. They are intended to show that faculty are actively participating in the live session rather than simply staying connected to the meeting.

## 8.3 Why are polls included in Standups?

Polls provide a simple way for faculty to respond, think about a question, and participate while the discussion is happening. They are included to encourage **engagement and active participation**, not to create a separate academic test.

## 8.4 Do I need to participate in the polls?

Yes. When a poll is presented during a Standup you are attending, you are expected to respond to it. Poll participation is part of the intended live-session experience and helps demonstrate active participation.

## 8.5 Is Standup completion based only on attendance minutes?

No. Both **total attendance time** and **number of sessions attended** matter, and participants are also expected to take part in the live-session polls. This gives the programme a better picture of sustained and active participation than attendance time alone.

## 8.6 Why is the requirement 720 minutes across at least 12 sessions?

The **720-minute** requirement ensures substantial overall live participation. The **12-session** requirement makes sure that participation is spread across multiple sessions rather than being completed through only a small number of unusually long meetings.

## 8.7 Is attendance calculated using a rolling window?

No. Standup attendance is **cumulative across your eight-week programme**. Your participation builds up from your own start date rather than being reset through separate rolling attendance windows.

## 8.8 Can a few long sessions replace the minimum number of sessions?

No. You need to meet both requirements: at least **720 minutes** and at least **12 sessions**.

## 8.9 What counts as attending a Standup session?

The current design uses **presence for at least half of the session** as the threshold for that session to count toward the 12-session requirement.

## 8.10 Are plenary and breakout minutes counted separately?

No. The programme does not use separate plenary and breakout attendance quotas. Standup attendance is based on your **total time in the meeting**.

## 8.11 Where does the poll data come from?

The initial implementation is designed around **Zoom poll data**. The platform is structured so that **Spandan** can be integrated later through the same underlying interface.

## 8.12 Will I be able to see my Standup progress?

Yes. The dashboard is designed to show your completed minutes, completed sessions, and available participation information so that you can see what you have completed and what remains.

## 8.13 What if my attendance record is incorrect?

The platform includes an unmatched-participant process so that attendance records are not silently lost when a participant cannot be confidently matched to a session. If you believe a record is wrong, report it through the available support or correction route.

## 8.14 Why are both minutes and session count required?

The two requirements measure different aspects of participation. Minutes measure the overall amount of live participation, while the session count ensures that participation is spread across the programme.

## 8.15 Do poll responses affect my academic or professional evaluation?

No. Polls are an **engagement and participation mechanism**, not a test of your teaching ability and not a separate academic grade.

## 8.16 Do I need to answer polls perfectly?

No. The purpose of the polls is to encourage active participation and engagement during the live discussion. Poll correctness is not presented as a separate academic grade.

## 8.17 Can I join a Standup late and still have it counted?

A session counts toward the 12-session requirement when you meet the programme's attendance threshold. Under the current design, that means being present for **at least half of the session**.

## 8.18 What happens if a session has no poll?

The absence of a poll should not reduce your attendance record. Poll participation is an engagement mechanism; it does not replace the underlying attendance requirement.

## 8.19 What should I expect to see on the dashboard?

The Standup section is designed to show:

1. **Minutes completed out of 720**
2. **Sessions completed out of 12**
3. **Participation information from the live sessions**

This allows you to identify early if you are falling behind instead of discovering it at the end of the eight weeks.

## 8.20 What if I miss some sessions during the programme?

Missing an occasional session does not automatically mean you cannot complete the component. You will still need to reach the required **720 minutes and 12 sessions** within your own eight-week programme period.

## 8.21 Can I make up for lower attendance later?

Yes, within your eight-week programme period. Because the attendance requirement is cumulative, further participation later can make up for an earlier period of lower attendance. What matters at completion is whether the required cumulative conditions have been met.

## 8.22 Is there a fixed weekly Standup schedule?

The programme specification defines the total live participation and minimum number of sessions, but it does not specify a fixed clock time in the FAQ source. The actual schedule should therefore be shared through the programme's session announcements and dashboard.

## 8.23 Are Standups mandatory for completing Guru Vani?

Yes. Standups are one of the required programme components, and the published attendance and participation requirements must be completed before the programme can be certified.

# 9. Vertical 3 — Activities and Teaching Cases

## 9.1 What are Activities?

Activities are short, teaching-focused case studies.

You respond to real classroom situations and then learn by reading and comparing other faculty responses.
## 9.2 How many cases do I need to complete?

You need **12 cases** to reach the required completion level.

Completing a case means reaching the programme's required final verdict, not merely opening or submitting the case. The 12-case requirement is therefore based on completed learning cycles.
## 9.3 When does a case count toward my 12?

A case counts only when all three conditions are true:

The three conditions work together: submission starts the process, the 7 peer picks provide the comparison activity, and the final verdict determines whether that case actually counts toward completion.

1. Your own response is submitted.
2. You complete **7 valid peer picks** on that case.
3. Your response reaches the **There yet** verdict.
## 9.4 What happens immediately after I submit a case?

Once you submit your response, that case moves from the **writing stage** into the **peer-review stage**. The review activity for that case unlocks immediately, so you can begin making your 7 required peer picks.

Your submission is therefore not the end of the activity. It is the point at which the second part of the learning cycle begins: reading other faculty responses, comparing them, and making reasoned judgements.

The platform tracks these stages separately so you can clearly see whether a case is submitted, being reviewed, awaiting a verdict, or completed.

Submitting your response moves that case into the peer-comparison stage. You do not have to wait for a separate manual approval before starting the review activity for that case.

You can then begin making the required peer comparisons.
## 9.5 Do I review my own response?

No.

Your 7 picks are made on **other participants' responses**.
## 9.6 What does “7 picks” mean?

For each completed case, you compare response pairs and select the stronger response according to the activity's comparison principles.

You need **7 valid picks**, each based on a comparison between anonymised responses.
## 9.7 Why use peer comparison instead of normal marks?

The activity is intended to develop professional judgement.

Instead of asking, “What score did I get?”, the experience asks you to read real responses and decide which one is stronger.

That comparison is itself part of the learning.
## 9.8 How many peer responses can I learn from?

Each participant can encounter multiple peer responses during their comparison cycles. The design is intended to expose participants to a range of approaches rather than a single model answer.
## 9.9 Are the peer responses anonymous?

The activity review design uses anonymised response pairs.

Participant names should not appear on the comparison screen or in the review record exposed to participants.
## 9.10 Can I see my review progress?

Yes.

The activity states include:

- **Not started**
- **Response submitted**
- **Reviewing — n of 7**
- **Awaiting verdict**
- **There yet**
- **Not yet**
## 9.11 What does “Awaiting verdict” mean?

It means you have completed your 7 picks, but the required peer reading for your response is still being completed.

It is **not a failure state**.
## 9.12 What does “There yet” mean?

It means your response has reached the required automatic verdict and the case counts toward your 12-case requirement.
## 9.13 What does “Not yet” mean?

It means your response has not yet reached the required verdict.

It is not the end of the activity.

You have two options:

1. **Revise and resubmit the same case** — the 7 completed picks are retained.
2. **Choose a different case** — this requires a new response and 7 new picks.
## 9.14 Can I resubmit unlimited times?

Yes.

While your 8-week programme is running, resubmission is unlimited.

There is no attempt penalty and the participant-facing design does not display an attempt count.
## 9.15 Do I have to repeat all 7 reviews after revising the same case?

No.

When you revise and resubmit the same case, the existing 7 picks are retained.
## 9.16 What if I choose a different case instead?

A different case requires a fresh response and **7 fresh picks**.
## 9.17 What happens if a response pair is impossible to judge?

You can flag the pair as unjudgeable.

That pair does not count toward your 7 picks, and you receive another pair.
## 9.18 What criteria should I use when comparing activity responses?

The activity comparison uses a three-part rubric:

1. **Fair to the other side** — this is the deciding consideration.
2. **Real reasons rather than “it depends”.**
3. **Did the classroom change take the other side seriously?**

This is the **Activities rubric**.

It is different from the Project rubric.
## 9.19 Does a case become complete simply because I submitted it?

No.

Submission alone is not enough.

You need the 7 valid picks and the **There yet** verdict as well.
## 9.20 Why is the verdict part of completion?

Because the programme wants the written response to matter.

The purpose is not only to count how much reviewing you did; it is also to develop the ability to construct and improve a strong response.
## 9.21 Is my case response protected while I am writing?

Yes. Case responses are completed in a focused submission environment so that participants can concentrate on the case before moving into peer comparison.
## 9.22 Does the platform control how long I spend reading peer responses?

The programme design includes a focused reading experience intended to ensure that participants spend meaningful time engaging with peer responses. Reading time can be tied to the amount of text being reviewed rather than allowing the comparison to become a superficial click-through.

## 9.23 How are peer responses matched for comparison?

The system is designed to use **anonymised response pairs**, avoid showing your own response, avoid repeating the same pair, randomise which response appears on each side, and prefer responses that have been read less often.
## 9.24 Why do I sometimes see an agreement figure after a pick?

The comparison screen can show how much other readers agreed with the choice for that particular pair. It is feedback about the comparison, not a numerical grade for your own case.
## 9.25 How is the Activities verdict decided?

The verdict is resolved automatically from the peer comparisons.

The design can stop early once enough evidence exists; otherwise it moves toward the full comparison count. At **14 comparisons**, the simple majority determines the verdict. An exact tie at 14 resolves to **There yet**.
## 9.26 What if two readers independently flag my response as unjudgeable?

The response can be withdrawn automatically and you are told to resubmit it. This is different from simply receiving a “Not yet” verdict.
## 9.27 Do peer reviews happen manually by a facilitator?

No. The peer matching, comparison flow, and ordinary verdict logic are designed to run automatically. Facilitators are available for exceptional support and audit rather than deciding every ordinary comparison.
## 9.28 Why are the activity responses compared in pairs?

Pairwise comparison asks the participant to make a concrete judgement: **which response is stronger, and why?**

This is different from giving every response an independent numerical score and is intended to strengthen professional judgement.
## 9.29 Will I see the same pair of responses twice?

The matching design is intended to avoid showing the same pair twice to the same judging sequence.

It also tries to prioritise responses that have been read less often so that attention is distributed across the response pool.
## 9.30 Does the side of the screen matter?

No.

The system randomises which response appears on which side so that “left” or “right” does not become a shortcut for choosing.
## 9.31 Why are response names hidden?

The activity is intended to focus the judgement on the **response itself**, not on the reputation, institution, seniority, or identity of the faculty member who wrote it.

That is why the comparison design uses anonymised response pairs.
## 9.32 What is the “agreement” number after a comparison?

It indicates how other readers judged that particular response pair.

It is feedback about the comparison you just made, not a grade for your response and not a programme score.
## 9.33 What happens if fewer faculty have submitted a case than needed for seven comparisons?

The case remains open until enough colleagues have submitted responses to make the review process possible.

The participant-facing message should explain that the case will complete when more colleagues have submitted, rather than making the participant think the system is broken.
## 9.34 What happens to an activity response that two independent readers consider unjudgeable?

The response can be withdrawn automatically and the author is informed to resubmit.

The goal is to protect the integrity of the comparison process rather than allowing an unjudgeable response to distort a verdict.
## 9.35 Is there a facilitator queue for ordinary activity verdicts?

No.

The design replaces standing manual verdict queues with automatic rules. Facilitators retain tools for exceptional support and audit rather than manually deciding every ordinary response.
## 9.36 What if my response is still being read near the end of my eight weeks?

The platform is designed to surface the “awaiting verdict” state rather than presenting it as a failure.

Because a late resubmission may need time to collect the required peer comparisons, the platform should warn participants when timing becomes tight.

No.

The peer matching, comparison flow, and automatic verdict logic are designed to run without requiring a facilitator to decide each case.

# 10. Vertical 4 — Final Project / Classroom Asset

## 10.1 What is the final project?

The final project is **one practical teaching artifact** that you can actually use in your classroom.

The best test is:

> **Could another teacher in the same subject pick it up and use it next term without you standing beside them?**
## 10.2 Is the project an essay about teaching?

No.

The project is not intended to be:

- An essay about teaching
- A reflection
- A literature review
- A research paper
- A full course syllabus

It should be a usable teaching asset.
## 10.3 What can I create?

Examples include:

1. A redesigned assessment for one unit
2. A session plan for one class
3. A project-based learning brief with scaffolding and assessment criteria
4. A rubric for an activity currently graded informally
5. A sequence of in-class activities for a difficult topic
6. A set of locally grounded cases or problems
7. A classroom intervention with a plan for checking whether it worked

These are examples, not a restrictive list. Another artifact is acceptable if it fits the same definition.
## 10.4 How long should the project be?

The suggested format is a **2–4 page document or slide deck**, plus a **cover note of about 200 words**.
## 10.5 What should the cover note include?

The cover note should briefly explain:

- The course and level
- Rough class size
- The classroom problem being addressed
- How you would know whether the artifact worked
## 10.6 Is the visual design of the project graded?

No.

The format is not assessed.

A clear, useful artifact in a simple document is better than a decorated artifact that is difficult to use.
## 10.7 How many peers review my project?

Each project is reviewed by **2 peers**.

Each participant also reviews **2 projects**.
## 10.8 Is project peer review anonymous?

The author's name is hidden from reviewers where possible, and the reviewer's name is hidden from the author unless the reviewer chooses to be identified. The teaching context can still reveal information about the author.

## 10.9 What criteria are used to review the project?

The project uses three criteria:

**1. Usable**
Could another teacher actually run it? Are the required materials present?

**2. Grounded**
Does it respond to a real classroom problem with enough context, rather than being a generic good idea?

**3. Honest about evidence**
Does it explain how the teacher would know whether the intervention worked — including what would count as it **not** working?

Each criterion is answered **Yes** or **Not yet**, with a short explanation.
## 10.10 Is the project rubric the same as the Activities rubric?

No.

They are intentionally separate.

- **Activities** use the three-line comparison rubric.
- **Project** uses **Usable, Grounded, and Honest about evidence**.
## 10.11 What is the “Ready / Not yet” outcome?

This is the project completion outcome.

A project is **Ready** when all three project criteria receive “Yes”.
## 10.12 What is National Repository nomination?

Reviewers also answer a separate question:

> Should this project be nominated for the National Repository?

This is a **recognition decision**, separate from the Ready/Not yet completion decision.
## 10.13 Does repository nomination affect my certificate?

No.

A project can be Ready and still not be nominated.

Repository nomination does **not** determine whether you complete the Project vertical or receive your certificate.
## 10.14 What happens if the two reviewers disagree?

A third review is drawn automatically.

Then **2 out of 3** reviews determine the outcome.

No facilitator needs to intervene in this process.
## 10.15 What happens if both reviewers say Ready?

The project resolves to **Ready**.
## 10.16 What happens if both reviewers say Not yet?

The project resolves to **Not yet** and the reviews are returned to the participant so the artifact can be revised and resubmitted.
## 10.17 Can I revise and resubmit my project?

Yes.

Revision and resubmission are unlimited while your 8-week programme is active.
## 10.18 Can a single reviewer permanently block my completion?

No.

The automatic resolution process and unlimited revision path are designed to prevent one peer judgement from permanently blocking a participant.
## 10.19 Does a human facilitator decide whether my project is Ready?

No. The Project peer-review outcome is resolved automatically according to the defined review rules. The core team only becomes involved when a Project is nominated for the National Repository.
## 10.20 Why does the project ask which of seven types it is closest to?

The list is there to help faculty recognise the kind of artifact they are creating.

It is not meant to restrict creativity. A different artifact is acceptable if it meets the core definition: a real teaching asset that can be used in practice.
## 10.21 Does my artifact have to be beautifully designed?

No.

The programme explicitly says that format is not assessed.

The priority is usefulness, clarity, grounding in a real classroom problem, and an honest account of how you would know whether the artifact worked.
## 10.22 Can my project be specific to my own institution?

Yes.

In fact, grounding the artifact in a real classroom and context is part of the review expectation.

A project does not need to be generic enough for every institution.
## 10.23 Why does the project ask how I will know whether it worked?

The programme is intended to support classroom application, not only idea generation.

The evidence criterion asks you to think ahead about what evidence would tell you the artifact is useful—and what evidence would tell you it is **not** working.
## 10.24 What happens if one project reviewer says Ready and another says Not yet?

A third review is automatically drawn.

The final outcome is determined by **2 of the 3 reviews**, so disagreement does not leave the participant waiting indefinitely for a facilitator decision.
## 10.25 What if both project reviewers say Not yet?

The project becomes **Not yet**, and the reviews are returned so you can revise and resubmit.

You can continue revising during your eight-week programme.
## 10.26 Does being nominated for the National Repository mean my project is automatically published?

No.

A nomination sends the project to the core team's repository queue.

The core team makes the publication decision.
## 10.27 Can a project be Ready but not nominated?

Yes.

That is expected.

“Ready” answers whether the artifact meets the completion standard. “Nominate” asks a different question: whether the artifact should be considered for wider national sharing.
## 10.28 Can a project be nominated before it reaches Ready?

A reviewer can make the separate nomination judgement, but repository selection does not replace the Project completion requirement. For certification, the artifact still has to reach **Ready** and the participant must complete their **two peer reviews**.

No.

The project peer-review outcome is automated according to the defined review logic.

The only human step in the Project vertical is the **National Repository core-team queue** for nominated work.

# 13. Certificate and Completion

## 13.1 What do I need to complete the programme?

You need all five completion requirements:

1. ViBe Coursework at **100%**
2. **720 minutes** across at least **12 Standup sessions**, with participation in the live-session polls
3. **12 Activities** reaching **There yet**
4. A Project reaching **Ready**, plus **2 peer reviews given**
5. A **verified NOC**

## 13.2 Is the certificate issued automatically?

Yes. Once all completion requirements are satisfied, the certificate is designed to issue automatically without a separate participant-side approval request.

## 13.3 What does the certificate say about learning hours?

The certificate records **33 notional learning hours** and is designed to include your own programme start and completion dates, along with a certificate number and public verification URL.

## 13.4 How should the NCRF wording be understood?

The programme may be described as: **“33 notional learning hours, equivalent to one NCRF credit.”**

This wording describes the programme's learning volume. It should not be described as “awarding,” “granting,” “carrying,” or being formally “accredited” for one NCRF credit, because formal NCRF approval is not in place.

## 13.5 Does the certificate depend on attendance alone?

No. Attendance is only one part of the completion requirements. Coursework, Activities, the Project, and verified NOC status are also required.

## 13.6 Does my Project have to be selected for the National Repository to receive the certificate?

No. Repository nomination is separate from programme completion. A Project can be **Ready** and still not be selected for wider publication.

## 13.7 Do my case attempts affect certificate eligibility?

No. The number of attempts is not a completion criterion. The requirement is to reach the required completion state.

## 13.8 Does my intake conversation score affect my certificate?

No. There is no merit score for the intake conversation. Its purpose is to understand the faculty member's context, and completion of the required conversation is what matters for the admission flow.

## 13.9 What if I am missing one requirement at the end?

The dashboard is designed to show which completion requirement is still incomplete and, where applicable, how much remains.

## 13.10 Is non-completion recorded?

Yes. Non-completion is recorded and may be taken into account when places are allocated in later cycles.

# 14. Privacy, Research and the Yaksha Conversation

## 14.1 Why is the intake conversation recorded?

The conversation helps the programme understand how faculty think about teaching, learning, classroom challenges, and professional development. It also gives the programme a systematic way to understand what faculty expect from professional development.

## 14.2 Am I told that the conversation is recorded?

Yes. Before the first turn, participants are informed in plain language that the conversation is recorded and may be used for research, along with information about whom to contact regarding withdrawal.

## 14.3 Can my transcript be used for research?

The programme informs participants that the conversation **may be used for research**, subject to the applicable consent and ethics requirements.

## 14.4 Is my identity removed from research exports?

The design calls for de-identification at capture. The transcript is stored against an opaque research ID, while the identity map is stored separately.

## 14.5 Is the transcript used to judge my teaching quality or admission merit?

No. The transcript is not read to decide whether you are a good faculty member or to assign an admission merit score. The admission flow depends on completion of the required intake conversation and NOC verification.

# 15. Common Misunderstandings

## 15.1 “I only need to watch the videos.”

No. Coursework is only one part of Guru Vani. You also need to complete the Standups, Activities, Project, and NOC requirements.

## 15.2 “I attended the sessions, so I completed Standups.”

Not necessarily. You must meet the required **720 minutes**, **12-session**, and live-session participation requirements. Polls are part of the engagement expected during Standups.

## 15.3 “If I submit 12 cases, I am done with Activities.”

No. A case counts only after your response is submitted, you complete **7 valid peer picks**, and the response reaches **There yet**.

## 15.4 “Not yet means I failed.”

No. **Not yet** is not a final rejection. You can revise and resubmit the same case, retaining the completed 7 picks, or choose a different case and complete a fresh set of 7 picks.

## 15.5 “The Project must be selected for the National Repository.”

No. Repository selection is separate from completion. Your Project can be **Ready** without being nominated for the repository.

## 15.6 “The Project rubric and Activities rubric are the same.”

No. Activities use their own comparison rubric, while Project review uses the criteria **Usable, Grounded, and Honest about evidence**.

## 15.7 “Yaksha is interviewing me for selection.”

No. The intake conversation is non-evaluative. There is no merit score based on your answers.

## 15.8 “I need the NOC before I can start.”

No. You can start after completing the application and intake conversation. The NOC can be submitted afterward and should be completed as soon as possible.

## 15.9 “Guru Vani has fixed cohort dates.”

No. Guru Vani uses rolling intake. Your eight-week programme starts from your own start date.

## 15.10 “A leaderboard position determines completion.”

No. Certification is based on the published completion requirements, not leaderboard position.

# 17. What Faculty Members Should Know Before Applying

## 17.1 What should I check before I apply?

Before starting Guru Vani, make sure the programme fits alongside your existing institutional responsibilities. In particular, plan for about **4 hours per week for 8 weeks**, including approximately **1.5 hours of live participation per week**.

You should also be prepared to complete the ViBe Coursework, participate in Standups and their live polls, complete 12 teaching cases, create a practical classroom Project, give 2 Project peer reviews, and arrange a verified NOC as soon as possible.

## 17.2 What is the biggest thing to understand about Guru Vani?

Guru Vani is not designed as “watch content → attend sessions → receive a certificate.” The programme asks faculty to **participate, judge, compare, revise, and create**.

## 17.3 What should I expect if something does not go as planned?

The platform is designed to distinguish between different situations rather than treating every delay as a failure. For example, an Activity can be **Awaiting verdict** while peers are still reading it, a **Not yet** response can be revised, and a delayed NOC can still be submitted after the first seven days.

## 17.4 What should I focus on throughout the eight weeks?

Participate consistently, take the peer-comparison work seriously, keep an eye on your dashboard, and use the programme to build something that is genuinely useful in your classroom.
